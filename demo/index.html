Step 01The threat

Nothing is on fire. That is the problem.

Two objects in low Earth orbit do not drift together. They arrive, at a combined 8,252 metres per second, and pass in under a second. There is no alarm, no impact you could react to. The only warning is arithmetic done hours in advance.

Propagating NOAA 20 forward six hours puts it 133.7 metres from a tracked fragment. Nobody would fly that.

Step 03The options

Twenty-five ways to not be there.

You cannot steer out of the way. You change your orbit slightly, hours ahead, and arrive somewhere else. The grid is four burn times, two directions, three magnitudes — 24 burns, plus doing nothing.

Each is propagated and screened against the object that triggered the alert. Twelve clear the floor. Rank them by fuel and the cheapest survivor is a 0.10 m/s retrograde burn thirty minutes in.

Step 04The trap

It works. Then it doesn’t.

Against the object that raised the alarm, the burn is a clean success: 133.7 m becomes 2,016.9 m. Every check it was asked to pass, it passes.

But it was only ever screened against one object. Ninety minutes after the encounter it was meant to fix, the same burn brings the spacecraft to 523.2 metres from a second object — closer than the floor it was trying to protect.

Step 05The answer

Spend more fuel. Check everything.

Doubling the burn to 0.20 m/s moves the spacecraft far enough that both encounters open up. A second, independent pass rebuilds the trajectory from the raw scenario, scans five times finer, and screens every object over the whole horizon — not just the one that raised the alarm.

That is the entire point. The cheapest option that satisfies the question you asked is not the same as the option that is safe.

Method

What is real, and what is not.

A demo that blurs this line is worthless. Every figure on this page is one of the four things on the right, and nothing is dressed up as more than it is.

Real
The orbit

NOAA 20 (JPSS-1), catalogue number 43013. The starting state is derived from a published element set at its own epoch — not a plausible-looking orbit, an actual one.

Real
The context figures

Tracked-object counts and the ISS manoeuvre threshold come from ESA’s Space Debris Office and NASA conjunction assessment practice.

Simulated
Both debris objects

Constructed backwards from an encounter we chose, then verified by propagating forward again. No real conjunction is depicted, and none of this is a prediction about any real object.

Computed
Every distance on this page

Two-body propagation with instantaneous impulses over a six-hour horizon. No drag, no oblateness, no finite burn duration, no navigation uncertainty — and no collision probability, which needs covariance data this model does not have.

Two-body simulation · 6-hour horizon · distances are centre-to-centre — Models enlarged for visibility. Positions and distances are computed.
